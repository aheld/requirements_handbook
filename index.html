A friendly handbook for requirements and user stories.

Good software starts with two things written down well: a clear requirement that says what we're building and why, and a clear user story that says who it's for and what value they get. This handbook is a working companion for the analysts who write both.

Two halves of the same job

Requirements and user stories aren't competing formats — they're different altitudes of the same conversation. Requirements live higher up: business goals, system behaviors, the qualities the solution must hold. User stories live closer to the ground: one specific user, doing one specific thing, getting one specific benefit. You'll move between them constantly.

REQUIREMENTS
What & why
Define the problem, capture the conditions the solution must meet, get sign-off from stakeholders. Higher-level, longer-lived.
USER STORIES
Who & what value
Translate requirements into small, shippable slices of user value. Lower-level, refined sprint to sprint.

Writing requirements, in one paragraph

A good requirement names a specific capability the system must have, written so anyone can test whether it's been met. Stay focused on what, not how. Be specific, measurable, and traceable back to a real stakeholder. Use concrete verbs (create, validate, notify) and avoid vague ones (manage, process, enhance). And remember: a requirement is a promise to deliver something — every sentence should be one someone could sign off on.

Requirements describe the system. Stories describe the person using it. You need both, and they should agree.

What's in here

Twelve short sections. We start with requirements fundamentals, then move into the day-to-day craft of user stories — writing them, checking them with INVEST, splitting them, and pulling out the hidden ones. A glossary at the end covers every term we use.

Mindset
A requirement is a promise to deliver. A user story is a promise to have a conversation. Both are documents — but neither is the work itself. The work is the conversations they unlock.
Requirements

Requirements 101

A user story is one kind of requirement, but it sits inside a wider family. Knowing the family helps you write better stories — and helps you escalate when a story is really hiding a business-level problem.

The 4D process

1 · DEFINE
Frame the problem
Situation, problem, objectives, scope, benefits, stakeholders. Nail these before anyone starts writing stories.
2 · DISCOVER
Elicit requirements
Interviews, workshops, observation, surveys, prototypes. Multiple techniques because no single one is enough.
3 · DOCUMENT
Rewrite, review, revise, repeat
First drafts are wrong; that's fine. Run them through the Top 10 checklist and iterate until they hold up.
4 · DELIVER
Inspect & sign off
Explicit acceptance from each impacted stakeholder. No tacit approvals — they unravel later.

Kinds of requirement

You'll hear all of these. They aren't interchangeable.

Requirement
A condition or capability needed by a stakeholder to solve a problem or achieve an objective. Must be measurable, bounded by constraints, and met by the solution.
Business Case
The justification for the project — value added, presented to management for a go/no-go decision. Usually contains an executive summary, current situation, problem statement, objectives, scope, benefits, and stakeholders.
Business Requirements
High-level statements of the goals, objectives, or needs of the enterprise. The "why we're doing this at all" layer.
Stakeholder Requirements
The needs of a particular stakeholder or class of stakeholders, and how that stakeholder will interact with the solution.
Functional Requirements
What the system actually does — the behaviors, operations, and information it manages. Most user stories live here.
Non-Functional Requirements
The qualities and conditions under which the solution must remain effective — performance, security, accessibility, reliability. Also called supplementary or quality requirements.

Why they're traps

"Manage users" could mean create, edit, delete, deactivate, audit, export — any combination. Pick the actual verbs you need. "The admin can create, edit, and deactivate user accounts" is testable; "manage users" is not.

What does processing mean? Read it? Validate it? Transform it? Write it somewhere? Each of those is a different requirement.

Tracking implies capture + store + display + maybe alert. Spell out which of those the system actually does.

Both are verbs of intention, not behavior. They describe what we hope the system feels like, not what it does. Strip them out and name the actual behaviors underneath.

Before
The system shall manage customer information.
After
The system shall create, update, retrieve, and delete customer records.
Why it's better: The 'after' tells engineering exactly what to build, and tells QA exactly what to test. The 'before' tells nobody anything.
Foundation

Anatomy of a user story

A user story is a short, simple description of a feature from the perspective of the person who'll use it. The whole point is to make the value to that person impossible to miss.

The template

Every story you write should fit into this shape:

As a type of user,
I want action they want to take,
so that benefit they get.

The three slots aren't decoration — each one prevents a specific failure mode:

  • "As a…" stops you from designing for "the user" in the abstract. There's always a specific persona.
  • "I want…" forces you to name a concrete action, not a feature or a screen.
  • "So that…" is the value statement. If you can't finish this clause, you don't have a story yet.

A real one

As a customer,
I want to filter products by price,
so that I can find items within my budget.

Notice how short that is. A good story is a sentence, not a page. Anything else — the design, the edge cases, the technical approach — belongs in conversations, comments, and acceptance criteria.

More good examples

The personas can be named individuals or roles — both work, as long as the persona is specific enough to picture:

  • As Max, I want to invite my friends, so we can enjoy this service together.
  • As Sascha, I want to organize my work, so I can feel more in control.
  • As a manager, I want to understand my colleagues' progress, so I can report our success and failures.
Features should support the user — by using functionality to do tasks and achieve goals. Not the other way around.

Tip
You don't have to drive every step. You do need to know who does, and what state the story should be in when it lands on their plate. If you don't know who owns the next step, that's your real bottleneck.

Where new analysts get stuck

Almost always one of three things: the value is fuzzy ("so that we improve the experience"), the scope is too big, or there are no acceptance criteria. Check those three before you re-submit.

If a developer could read it without you in the room and form a reasonable opinion about scope, it's ready. If they'd need to ask three clarifying questions just to size it, it isn't.

Acceptance is shared. The PO confirms it matches what they asked for. The BA confirms it matches the documented requirements. The business requestor confirms it actually solves their problem. All three matter.

Writing

Writing tips

The mechanics of a good story are mostly about restraint. Say less. Be specific. Leave room for conversation. Here are the seven moves that separate a story that ships from one that stalls.

1. Use the template

You will be tempted to drop "As a… I want… so that…" once you're experienced. Don't. The template's job is to force the value statement, and that pressure is exactly what makes stories good.

Before
Add price filter to product listing page.
After
As a customer, I want to filter products by price, so that I can find items within my budget.
Why it's better: The 'before' is a task. The 'after' is a story — it names who benefits and why.

2. Focus on the user, not the feature

The unit of value is "a thing a real person can do," not "a thing the engineering team can build."

Before
As a user, I want a dropdown for country selection.
After
As a first-time shopper, I want to set my country so prices and shipping show in terms I recognize.
Why it's better: A dropdown is one possible solution. The real story is the underlying need — and there might be a better way to meet it.

3. Keep it simple and concise

Plain language. No technical jargon. If you're tempted to write a second paragraph, you probably have a second story.

Before
As a user, I want to leverage our enhanced authentication paradigm via OAuth 2.0 with PKCE to securely federate identity across our ecosystem.
After
As a returning customer, I want to sign in with Google, so I don't have to remember another password.
Why it's better: Same underlying work. The 'after' actually tells you why we're doing it.

4. Always name the benefit

"So that…" is the most important part of the sentence. If you can only write "so that the user can do X" without saying why X matters, the story isn't ready.

Before
As a user, I want to receive email notifications, so that I get email notifications.
After
As a project manager, I want to be notified when a teammate finishes a task, so I can hand off the next piece of work without checking the board.
Why it's better: The 'after' tells you the real job. Now a developer can suggest in-app or SMS as alternatives — and the story still makes sense.

5. Include acceptance criteria

The story sets the direction. Acceptance criteria make it testable. We give them their own section — see Acceptance criteria.

6. Prioritize honestly

Rank stories by importance and urgency. Use frameworks where they help — Weighted Shortest Job First, business value, dependency order — but the goal is just: which thing pays off most for the least work, soonest?

7. Collaborate, document, align

A story written alone in a doc is a draft. A story that's been talked through with users, the PO, engineering, and QA, and where everyone has agreed in writing — that's ready. Document who weighed in, and make sure impacted stakeholders explicitly signed off.

The two-minute rule
If you can't read your story out loud in under two minutes and have a developer nod, simplify or split it. Length is almost always a smell.

When to use it
Run INVEST right before a story goes into a refinement or sprint planning meeting. If any letter fails, the story isn't ready — fix it before it eats team time.

Writing

Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria are the conditions that have to be true for the story to be considered done. They turn "I think this is finished" into "we agreed this is finished" — and they're how QA writes tests without guessing.

Three rules

  1. State the intent, not the solution. "The user can choose a course" beats "the user can select a course from a dropdown." The dropdown is a design decision; the choice is the requirement.
  2. Stay relatively high level. Not every detail belongs in writing. Cover the five Ws — who, what, when, where, why — and let the rest live in conversation.
  3. Be implementation-agnostic. The statement should hold whether the feature ends up on web, mobile, or desktop.

The Given / When / Then format

The most common format. It reads naturally and maps cleanly to automated tests:

Given some starting context,
when the user does this thing,
then this outcome should happen.

A worked example

Take the story we keep coming back to:

As a customer,
I want to filter products by price,
so that I can find items within my budget.

Good acceptance criteria for that story:

  • Given I am on the product page, when I select a price range, then I should see only products within that range.
  • Given I am on the product page, when I reset the filter, then I should see all products.

Before and after

Before
The filter should work properly.
After
Given I am on the product page, when I select the $0–$50 range, then only products priced $0–$50 inclusive should be visible.
Why it's better: 'Works properly' isn't testable. The 'after' is a concrete check QA can write tomorrow.
Before
User can select a course from a dropdown menu.
After
The user can choose a course.
Why it's better: The original baked in the UI. The rewrite lets design make that call — and stays true whether we ship a dropdown, a search, or autocomplete.
Refining

Splitting & story mapping

The two most useful refinement techniques: zooming out to see the whole user journey, and zooming in to break a too-big story into shippable pieces.

Story mapping

Lay out the user journey end-to-end, then map stories onto each step. The big wins:

  • You see the gaps — places in the journey that nobody has written a story for.
  • You see the order — which stories block which other stories.
  • You see the minimum slice — what's the smallest version of this journey that's still usable?

To achieve their goals, users perform activities, which are made of tasks. Story mapping keeps you on the user's verbs, not the software's nouns.

Splitting large stories

If a story can't be finished in a sprint, split it. The trick is splitting along value lines, not technical layers.

Before
  • Build database schema
  • Build API endpoint
  • Build UI
After
  • As a shopper, I can filter by price range
  • As a shopper, I can filter by brand
  • As a shopper, I can combine filters
Why it's better: Splitting by technical layer means nothing ships until all three are done. Splitting by user-facing value means each piece is independently demoable.

Use real examples

Pin a real-world scenario to the story — a specific customer, a specific purchase, a specific journey. It anchors the conversation in something everyone can picture and exposes edge cases the abstract version hid.

Heuristic
If a story takes longer than 15 minutes to walk through at refinement, split it. If it takes 30 seconds, it's probably right.
Refining

Finding hidden stories

Most of the stories you'll miss aren't hard — they're just things you didn't think to ask about. These six prompts surface them. Run through them every time you finish a first draft of a feature.

What will users most likely want to do next?
What mistakes could a user make here?
What happens in the event of an error?
What could confuse a user at this point?
What other information would a user want — or could use?
How will I demo this story?
Why 'how will I demo it' matters
Asking how you'd demo the story forces you to picture the finished thing. If you can't picture a believable demo in 60 seconds, the story is too vague or too big.

Other strategies for keeping stories sharp

Stories rot. Continuously review and refine them with the team so they stay relevant and clear. Reality changes between when you write a story and when it ships — let the story keep up.

Regularly update the backlog to re-rank and refine stories based on new feedback and changing priorities. A long-untouched backlog is almost always wrong.

Get the work in front of real users as fast as you reasonably can. Then turn what they say back into new stories. The feedback loop is the actual product process; everything else is plumbing.

Bring development, design, QA, and whoever else into story creation. Every function spots a different category of gap. Solo-authored stories almost always have blind spots.

Jira, Adonis, and Miro help with organization, collaboration, and tracking — and they bake in process flow. Pick the one your team will actually keep up to date. A pristine tool nobody uses is worse than a messy one everyone does.

Glossary

The terms used in this handbook, defined in plain language. Keep this page open in a tab during your first few weeks.

User Story
A short description of a feature from the user's perspective, in the form: As a [user], I want [action], so that [benefit].
Acceptance Criteria
The specific, testable conditions that must be true for a story to be considered done.
INVEST
A six-letter check for story quality: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
SMART
A five-letter check for individual requirements: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Traceable.
Backlog
The ordered list of all the work the team could do — stories, bugs, tasks — ranked by priority.
Backlog grooming
Regular work on the backlog to keep it ranked, refined, and relevant. Also called backlog refinement.
Refinement
The session where the team reviews upcoming stories, asks clarifying questions, and makes sure they're ready for sprint planning.
Story mapping
A technique for laying out user journeys end-to-end so you can see gaps, order, and the smallest usable slice.
Persona
A specific imagined user — named or role-based — used to ground a story in someone real.
Functional requirement
Something the system does — a behavior, action, or operation.
Non-functional requirement
A quality the system must have — performance, security, reliability, accessibility. Also called supplementary or quality requirements.
Business case
The written justification for a project, used to get a go/no-go decision from leadership.
Given/When/Then
A format for acceptance criteria: starting context, user action, expected outcome.
WSJF
Weighted Shortest Job First — a prioritization method that ranks work by value divided by effort.
PO / BA / QA
Product Owner, Business Analyst, Quality Assurance. The three roles most often involved in shaping and accepting a story.
